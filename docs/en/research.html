SUPERNOVA THEORY · PROJECT 01

Supernova fallback

Not all material launched by an exploding star escapes into space. By following the matter that returns to the centre, I study how neutron stars and black holes acquire their final masses.

In a core-collapse supernova, part of the ejecta can fall back onto the newly formed compact object. This fallback helps determine the final mass of a neutron star or black hole. Using one-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations, I compared progenitors that differ only in whether they retain their hydrogen envelope. The calculations reveal a sharp transition: when the explosion energy is approximately two to three times the binding energy of the hydrogen envelope, the reverse shock switches between returning to the centre and escaping outwards. This changes the remnant mass by more than two solar masses. Progenitors with different initial masses follow a common relation once the explosion energy is normalized by the envelope binding energy, providing a compact prescription for population-synthesis calculations.

Question

How does the hydrogen envelope alter supernova fallback?

Method

One-dimensional models differing only in the hydrogen envelope

Key result

A universal mass transition set by the envelope binding energy

The hydrogen envelope launches a reverse shock

01 Before entering the hydrogen envelope, the shocks evolve almost identically. Inside the envelope, a reverse shock develops and drives matter back towards the centre.

Explosion strength selects the remnant mass

02 Every progenitor from 18 to 28 solar masses exhibits a sharp transition in remnant mass. Its location is set by roughly two to three times the hydrogen-envelope binding energy.

Why this matters

The hydrogen envelope is not merely the outermost part of the star. Through the reverse shock, it can substantially alter the compact object left at the centre. Its binding energy therefore offers a practical way to predict the remnant mass after fallback.

FAILED SUPERNOVAE · PROJECT 02

Failed supernovae: Finding black-hole birth in dust

I use the infrared light left after a star disappears to uncover the birth of a black hole.

Some massive stars are expected to collapse into black holes without producing a luminous explosion. These events are known as “failed supernovae.” A key to finding them is infrared emission from dust that condenses in the small amount of material that escapes. I constructed a time-dependent model linking the ejecta’s thermal evolution, dust nucleation and growth, optical properties, and radiative transfer. In representative low-energy ejecta, dust forms efficiently from roughly 1000 days after the explosion, absorbs the central source, and reradiates its energy in the mid-infrared, imprinting a silicate feature near 10 µm. At approximately 4000 days, the model spectrum broadly agrees with JWST observations of the candidate M31-2014-DS1. The result shows how long-term infrared monitoring and mid-infrared spectroscopy can reveal black-hole formation that is difficult to detect in visible light.

Question

How can infrared afterglows reveal optically dark black-hole formation?

Method

Time-dependent thermal, dust-formation, opacity, and radiative-transfer modelling

Key result

A 10 µm silicate feature and a long-lived infrared afterglow

Dust transforms central light into infrared emission

01 Low-energy ejecta expand slowly and remain dense as they cool, allowing dust to form efficiently from roughly 1000 days. The dust reprocesses central light into the mid-infrared and produces a characteristic silicate structure near 10 µm.

Reproducing the candidate’s JWST spectrum

02 The model at approximately 4000 days (left) broadly reproduces the infrared luminosity, continuum, and structure around 10 µm observed by JWST for M31-2014-DS1 (right).

Why this matters

The onset of infrared brightening marks when the ejecta cool to the dust-formation temperature, not the instant when the black hole forms. Combining optical fading, the infrared SED, and composition-sensitive absorption features can constrain both the collapse time and the properties of the ejecta.
